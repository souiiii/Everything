# Financial Markets — Foundations & Securities Market

These are cumulative notes for the financial-markets learning track. The aim is to understand **why markets exist, how capital moves through them, who the important participants are, and what risks each part of the system creates**. Procedures, application timelines, and other rules that can change over time are deliberately left out unless they are needed to understand the concept.

---

# Part I — Foundations

## 1. What financial markets actually do

Financial markets move money from people and institutions that have **capital available now** to people, companies, and governments that can **use that capital now**.

The simplest mental model is:

**Savers / investors → financial system → users of capital**

The saver gives up the use of money today and accepts some risk. In return, the saver expects compensation through interest, dividends, or a possible increase in the value of the asset owned. The borrower or company receives access to money earlier, which can allow productive activity to happen now rather than years later.

This is the basic economic purpose behind finance. Markets are not primarily about speculation or watching prices move on a screen. Their deeper role is to **allocate savings, capital, and risk across people, institutions, and time**.

## 2. Why money today can matter more than money later

A company may prefer to raise money today rather than wait many years to accumulate enough cash internally. If capital is available now, it can be used to build a factory, buy equipment, hire employees, fund research, enter a new market, or expand an existing business.

Waiting has a cost because opportunities are not permanent. Demand can change, competitors can move first, technology can become obsolete, and regulations can alter the economics of a project. Access to capital therefore allows a business to act before it would have been able to using only retained earnings.

This gives a useful first intuition for finance: **capital moves purchasing power through time**. Investors postpone some consumption today so that another party can put those resources to productive use now, with the expectation that value will be returned later.

## 3. The main actors in the financial system

**Households and other savers** have money they do not need to spend immediately and may want that money to grow. They can keep it with banks or invest through securities and investment products.

**Companies and other borrowers** need capital for expansion, investment, working capital, acquisitions, or other productive activity. They may borrow from banks or raise money in securities markets.

**Banks** stand between savers and borrowers. A saver does not need to personally investigate every borrower because the bank performs credit assessment, manages a portfolio of loans, and takes responsibility for the lending relationship.

**Securities markets** provide another channel. Instead of relying only on a bank loan, an issuer can raise money from a broad set of investors by issuing securities such as shares or bonds.

**Governments** also participate in financial markets. They tax, spend, regulate financial activity, and can borrow by issuing debt securities when expenditure is not fully covered by current revenue.

**Insurers and investment institutions** play different roles in moving and pooling risk and capital. Mutual funds, for example, pool money from many investors and invest it in a portfolio of securities.

## 4. Why both banks and securities markets exist

Banks and securities markets solve related problems but do so in different ways.

A saver may prefer a bank because the saver does not want to evaluate each borrower individually. The bank performs that screening and manages credit risk on behalf of depositors. The saver therefore has a claim on the bank rather than a direct claim on each person or business that ultimately receives a loan.

A company may use securities markets when it wants access to a broad pool of investors, particularly when it needs a large amount of long-term capital or wants an alternative to conventional bank borrowing. Through the securities market, investors can hold claims more directly on the issuer.

The useful distinction is:

**Bank finance = an intermediary stands between saver and borrower.**  
**Securities-market finance = issuers raise money by selling securities to investors.**

Neither channel makes the other unnecessary. Real financial systems use both because different borrowers, investors, risks, and funding needs are better served by different structures.

## 5. Shares and bonds represent different claims

### Shares

Buying a share means buying an **ownership claim** in a company. A shareholder participates in the economic success or failure of the business. Returns may come from dividends distributed by the company and from a capital gain if the share is later sold for more than its purchase price.

A company does not promise that the market price of its shares will rise, and profits do not automatically have to be distributed as dividends. Equity holders therefore accept substantial uncertainty in exchange for participation in the upside of the business.

### Bonds

Buying a bond means **lending money to the issuer**. The issuer normally promises defined payments, such as periodic interest and repayment of principal at maturity. The investor is therefore a creditor rather than an owner.

The simplest distinction is:

**Share = ownership claim.**  
**Bond = debt claim.**

## 6. Why bonds are often less risky than shares of the same company

A bond issued by a company is usually less risky than that company's equity because the bondholder has a contractual claim for repayment, while the shareholder has no guaranteed return. If the company fails, creditors generally rank ahead of shareholders when the remaining assets are distributed. Shareholders are residual owners and therefore absorb losses after higher-priority claims have been considered.

That does not make bonds risk-free. The issuer can default, bond prices can fall when market interest rates change, and inflation can reduce the purchasing power of future fixed payments.

The important principle is that **higher perceived risk normally requires a higher expected return to persuade investors to bear that risk**.

## 7. Coupon and yield are related, but they are not the same thing

There is no universal return that a bond must offer. The rate demanded by investors depends on the issuer's creditworthiness, the maturity of the bond, prevailing interest rates, inflation expectations, currency risk, and other market conditions.

The **coupon rate** is the stated interest rate attached to the bond. The **yield** reflects the return implied by the price at which the bond is currently bought and the cash flows the investor expects to receive. Once a bond begins trading, its market price can move, so its current yield need not equal its original coupon rate.

For now, the important point is simply that **coupon describes the promised payment structure, while yield describes the return implied by the investment's current price and future payments**.

# Part II — Securities Market Structure

## 8. What the securities market is really for

A securities market is a mechanism through which issuers can raise funds by issuing securities and investors can later buy and sell those securities. Its most important economic function is the **allocation of savings**: money held by investors can be directed toward companies, governments, and other entities that need capital.

This creates a two-sided relationship. Investors provide capital because they expect some economic benefit, such as dividends, interest, or capital appreciation. Issuers gain access to funds that can be used for productive purposes. When capital is directed toward useful businesses and projects, the market contributes to wider economic activity rather than leaving all savings idle.

The key idea is therefore broader than stock trading:

**Savings become investment capital, and securities are the claims that connect the two sides.**

## 9. Equity shares as securities

An equity share represents a portion of ownership in a company. The investor becomes a shareholder and can participate in the company's economic outcomes. Shareholders may receive dividends when the company chooses to distribute profits, and they may also have voting rights in important company decisions.

The value of an equity investment is not fixed. If the company's prospects improve, investors may be willing to pay more for its shares; if the business deteriorates, the price can fall. This is why equity offers both ownership participation and exposure to business risk.

The important mental model is that **a share is not simply a price that moves on an exchange; it is a legal and economic claim on a business**.

## 10. Mutual funds pool investors rather than making each investor build a portfolio alone

A mutual fund collects money from many investors and uses the combined pool to invest in securities such as shares, bonds, money-market instruments, or other permitted assets.

This changes the investor's relationship with the underlying assets. Instead of personally selecting and holding every security in the portfolio, the investor owns units of the fund, while the fund's portfolio is managed according to the scheme's stated investment objective.

The useful intuition is:

**Many investors → pooled fund → portfolio of securities**

Pooling can make diversification and professional portfolio management easier to access. It does not remove investment risk, because the value of the underlying portfolio can still rise or fall.

## 11. Primary and secondary markets solve different parts of the capital-market problem

The securities market is commonly divided into the **primary market** and the **secondary market**. They are closely connected, but the economic transaction taking place in each is different.

### Primary market — creation and issue of securities

The primary market is the **new-issues market**. Securities are being offered by an issuer as part of a capital-raising or ownership-sale event. This is the stage at which securities enter the public market.

When a company creates and sells **new shares**, the money paid by investors goes to the company. The company can then use that capital for the purpose for which the issue was made.

### Secondary market — trading securities that already exist

After securities have been issued and listed, investors can trade them with other investors in the secondary market. The company is normally not receiving the money from each ordinary secondary-market trade. The buyer pays the seller, ownership changes hands, and the security continues to exist.

This distinction is one of the most important in market structure:

| Question | Primary market | Secondary market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is happening? | A security is being issued or initially offered. | An already-issued security is being traded. |
| Who is central to the transaction? | Issuer and investors. | Buyers and sellers in the market. |
| Does an ordinary trade raise fresh money for the company? | A fresh issue can. | No. The money normally goes to the selling investor. |
| Why is it economically useful? | Capital formation and distribution of new securities. | Liquidity, continuous transfer of ownership, and market pricing. |

## 12. Who issues securities?

The SEBI booklet gives two broad issuer categories that are useful at this stage.

**Corporate entities** can issue equity securities such as shares and debt securities such as bonds or debentures. A company may therefore raise capital either by selling an ownership claim or by borrowing from investors.

**Central and State Governments** issue debt securities rather than equity in themselves. Government borrowing creates a debt claim; investors do not become owners of the government in the way shareholders become owners of a company.

This distinction helps prevent a common conceptual mistake: **not every issuer can issue every type of security**.

## 13. An IPO can contain a fresh issue, an offer for sale, or both

An **Initial Public Offer, or IPO**, is the first public offer of a company's shares. The important detail is that not all shares sold in an IPO necessarily raise fresh capital for the company.

In a **fresh issue**, the company creates new shares and sells them to public investors. The money paid for those shares goes to the company, increasing the capital available to the business.

In an **Offer for Sale, or OFS**, existing shareholders such as promoters or financial investors sell some of the shares they already own. The buyer's money goes to those selling shareholders rather than to the company.

So the phrase “the company raised the entire IPO amount” can be misleading unless the structure is checked. If an IPO contains both a fresh issue and an OFS, only the fresh-issue portion directly brings new money into the company.

A compact way to remember it is:

**Fresh issue → new shares → money to company.**  
**OFS → existing shares → money to selling shareholders.**

## 14. Why the secondary market matters even though it does not normally fund the company directly

At first, the secondary market can seem economically less important because ordinary trades simply move existing securities from one investor to another. Its real importance is **liquidity and continuous marketability**.

An investor is more willing to buy a security in the primary market if there is a credible way to sell it later. A functioning secondary market therefore makes primary-market investing more attractive because investors are not necessarily locked into the asset indefinitely.

Secondary trading also creates continuously observed market prices. Those prices reflect the changing willingness of buyers and sellers to transact and become useful information for investors, companies, and the wider financial system.

The two markets therefore reinforce each other: **the primary market creates securities and raises capital; the secondary market gives those securities an ongoing market after issuance**.

## 15. Market Infrastructure Institutions are the operating backbone of the market

A securities market needs more than buyers and sellers. Trades have to be entered, matched, settled, and recorded reliably. The institutions that provide this essential infrastructure are commonly called **Market Infrastructure Institutions, or MIIs**.

The three important types in this block are stock exchanges, clearing corporations, and depositories.

### Stock exchanges — where orders meet

A stock exchange provides the organized electronic trading platform on which securities can be bought and sold through registered market participants. It gives buyers and sellers a common venue, provides rules for trading, and allows prices to be formed through market activity.

A stock exchange should therefore be understood as **market infrastructure**, not as the owner of the securities being traded and not as the counterparty that permanently buys everything investors want to sell.

### Clearing corporations — making sure completed trades actually settle

Executing a trade is not the end of the process. Once a buyer and seller agree on a transaction, securities still have to reach the buyer and money still has to reach the seller.

A clearing corporation sits in the post-trade process and is responsible for clearing and settlement assurance. Its role is to make the market dependable by ensuring that completed exchange trades can proceed to settlement rather than leaving each buyer and seller to manage counterparty risk independently.

The clean mental distinction is:

**Exchange = trade execution and market venue.**  
**Clearing corporation = clearing, risk management, and settlement assurance.**

### Depositories — electronic ownership records

A depository holds securities in dematerialized, electronic form and maintains the system through which ownership can be recorded and transferred. Investors normally access depository services through a **Depository Participant, or DP**.

The relationship is similar in spirit to a banking network: the depository is the central infrastructure, while the DP is the investor-facing participant through which services such as maintaining a demat account are provided.

The depository therefore solves a different problem from the exchange. The exchange facilitates the trade; the depository infrastructure helps maintain and transfer the electronic securities ownership associated with that trade.

## 16. Market intermediaries connect investors and issuers to that infrastructure

Market intermediaries are participants that help the primary and secondary markets function smoothly. They perform specialized activities around issuing, buying, selling, recording, and servicing securities.

A **stock broker** gives investors access to the trading system and executes their buy or sell orders. A **Depository Participant** provides the investor-facing connection to depository services. **Merchant bankers** assist issuers with activities connected to raising securities-market capital. **Registrars and share-transfer agents** support issue and investor-record processes.

The important distinction is that an intermediary is not the same thing as the market infrastructure itself. A broker uses the exchange; a DP connects an investor to the depository. Keeping these roles separate makes the entire market structure easier to reason about.

## 17. One end-to-end mental model of a secondary-market trade

Suppose Investor A wants to buy shares and Investor B wants to sell them.

Investor A and Investor B submit orders through their brokers. Those orders reach the stock exchange, where compatible buy and sell orders can be matched. Once a trade is executed, the clearing and settlement system determines and manages the obligations created by that trade. The depository side then supports the transfer of the securities ownership records, while the corresponding money moves through the settlement process.

The important sequence is:

**Investor → broker → exchange → clearing/settlement → depository ownership transfer**

This is a conceptual map rather than a complete operational description. For placement and finance understanding, the goal is to know **why each layer exists** rather than to memorize changing procedural details.

## 18. Liquidity risk and business risk are different risks

### Liquidity risk

**Liquidity risk** is the risk that an investment cannot be bought or sold quickly enough at a reasonable price. An asset can be valuable on paper and still be difficult to convert into cash when the investor needs to exit.

For example, if very few buyers are willing to trade a particular security, a seller may have to wait or accept a significantly lower price. Liquidity is therefore about **the ease and cost of transacting**, not simply whether the underlying company is good or bad.

### Business risk

**Business risk** arises from adverse developments in the company itself. Operations can weaken, demand can fall, costs can rise, financing can become difficult, or other unfavorable business conditions can reduce the firm's ability to perform.

This is conceptually different from liquidity risk. A strong company can still have an illiquid security, and a highly liquid share can still belong to a business whose fundamentals are deteriorating.

A useful comparison is:

| Risk | Core question |
| --- | --- |
| Liquidity risk | **Can I transact in this security quickly and at a reasonable price?** |
| Business risk | **Can the underlying business continue to operate and perform successfully?** |

Keeping risks separated like this is important because different risks require different responses. A single phrase such as “this stock is risky” hides too much information to be useful.

# Recall Sheet

1. **What problem do financial markets solve?**  
   They connect capital that is available now with people and institutions that can use it now, while allocating risk and future financial claims.

2. **Why might a company raise money instead of waiting to save it internally?**  
   Access to capital today can allow productive investment to begin earlier and can prevent valuable opportunities from being lost while the company waits.

3. **Why do securities markets exist alongside banks?**  
   Banks intermediate lending, while securities markets give issuers another route to obtain capital from a broad pool of investors through tradable financial claims.

4. **What is the economic purpose of the securities market?**  
   It channels savings toward entities that need funds and creates securities that represent the resulting ownership or debt claims.

5. **What is the difference between a share and a bond?**  
   A share is an ownership claim. A bond is a debt claim in which the investor lends money to the issuer.

6. **What is a mutual fund?**  
   It is a pooled investment vehicle that combines money from many investors and invests that pool in a portfolio of securities according to the fund's mandate.

7. **Primary market vs secondary market?**  
   The primary market is where securities are issued or initially offered. The secondary market is where already-issued securities are subsequently traded among investors.

8. **What are the two broad issuer categories in the SEBI material?**  
   Corporate entities, which can issue equity and debt, and Central or State Governments, which issue debt securities.

9. **What is an IPO?**  
   It is the first public offer of a company's shares. It can include newly issued shares, existing shares sold by current shareholders, or both.

10. **Fresh issue vs Offer for Sale?**  
    In a fresh issue, new shares are created and the money goes to the company. In an OFS, existing shareholders sell shares they already own and the money goes to those sellers.

11. **Why is the secondary market economically important?**  
    It provides liquidity and continuing marketability for issued securities, which makes investors more willing to provide capital in the first place.

12. **What are the three MIIs covered here?**  
    Stock exchanges provide the trading venue, clearing corporations support clearing and settlement assurance, and depositories maintain securities in electronic form and support ownership transfer.

13. **What is the difference between a depository and a DP?**  
    The depository is the central securities-holding infrastructure; a Depository Participant is the investor-facing intermediary through which depository services are accessed.

14. **Liquidity risk vs business risk?**  
    Liquidity risk concerns the difficulty or cost of buying or selling the security. Business risk concerns deterioration in the operations or financial condition of the underlying company.
