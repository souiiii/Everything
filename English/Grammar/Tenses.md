## 1. Present Continuous (Present Progressive)

### 1.1 Formal definition

The **present continuous tense**, also called the *present progressive*, is a verb form used primarily to describe an action or situation that is in progress **at the moment of speaking** or **during a period around the present**. The action or situation is viewed as ongoing, rather than simply as a fact, routine, or completed event.

Although the tense contains the word *present*, it is not limited to events taking place at the exact second someone speaks. English speakers also use it for temporary circumstances, for situations changing over time, and—when the context establishes a plan—for definite future arrangements.

Compare these two sentences:

- **“I am reading a novel.”** The reading is an ongoing activity. I might be holding the book now, or I might be describing what I have been doing during this period.
- **“I read novels.”** This describes a general habit or usual behavior rather than emphasizing an activity in progress.

The difference is often one of **how the speaker presents the situation**: as unfolding now or around now, rather than as a regular fact.

### 1.2 Formal structure: *be* + present participle

The present continuous is constructed with the **present tense of the auxiliary verb *be*** followed by the **present participle** of the main verb, usually its **-ing form**.

**Formula:** Subject + am / is / are + base verb + -ing.

| Subject | Auxiliary | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | am | **I am studying** English. |
| He / she / it | is | **She is singing** on stage. |
| You / we / they | are | **They are preparing** dinner. |

The auxiliary changes to agree with the subject; the **-ing** form does not change according to the subject. Thus, *she is working* and *they are working* share the same main-verb form.

Contractions are ordinary in speech and informal writing: **I'm**, **he's**, **she's**, **it's**, **you're**, **we're**, and **they're**. In formal writing, the complete forms are equally correct.

#### Affirmative sentences

An affirmative sentence states that an action or situation is in progress.

**Pattern:** Subject + am/is/are + verb-ing + remainder of the sentence.

- **I am learning cloud computing this month.** The speaker is describing an activity taking place during the current period, not necessarily at every moment of the month.
- **Tod is playing for the football team this year.** The sentence presents team participation as a current-season situation.
- **The children are playing outside.** The activity is happening now.

**Common mistake:** *“She singing at the concert.”* is not a complete standard present-continuous construction. It needs the auxiliary: **“She is singing at the concert.”**

#### Negative sentences

To make the tense negative, place **not** immediately after **am, is, or are**.

**Pattern:** Subject + am/is/are + not + verb-ing.

- **Jimmy is not performing well this semester.** His performance is being assessed over the present semester, rather than in one instant.
- **I am not working on that feature today.**
- **They are not waiting for us.**

**Is not** is commonly shortened to **isn't**, and **are not** to **aren't**. In standard English there is no equivalent widely used contraction of *am not* to *amn't*; **I'm not** is the ordinary form.

**Common mistake:** *“He doesn't studying.”* incorrectly combines the present-simple auxiliary **does** with an **-ing** verb. The correct present-continuous form is **“He isn't studying.”** For the ordinary present simple, the correct form is **“He doesn't study.”**

#### Yes/no questions and short answers

In a present-continuous question, the auxiliary **comes before the subject**.

**Pattern:** Am/Is/Are + subject + verb-ing + … ?

- **Are you studying now?**
- **Is she performing at the event tomorrow?**
- **Aren't you coming along with us?**

A short answer normally uses the auxiliary again: **“Yes, I am,” “No, I'm not,” “Yes, she is,”** or **“No, she isn't.”** We usually do not say *“Yes, I am studying”* unless we want to repeat or emphasize the full action.

Negative questions such as **“Aren't you coming?”** are natural when the speaker expects that someone may come, or is surprised that they might not. This differs slightly in tone from the neutral question **“Are you coming?”**

**Common mistake:** *“You are coming with us?”* can function as a confirmation question in conversation, but the usual neutral question form is **“Are you coming with us?”**

#### Questions beginning with a question word

Place **what, why, where, when, how,** or another suitable question phrase before the auxiliary.

**Pattern:** Question word + am/is/are + subject + verb-ing + … ?

- **What are you reading?**
- **Why is she leaving early?**
- **Where are they staying this week?**

**Common mistake:** *“What you are doing?”* has statement order. The standard direct question is **“What are you doing?”**

### 1.3 Writing the -ing form correctly

Most verbs simply add **-ing**: *read → reading*, *play → playing*, and *work → working*. Some verbs require spelling changes, but the tense itself remains the same.

| Basic spelling rule | Examples | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usually add **-ing**. | read → reading; sing → singing | The original spelling remains unchanged. |
| Drop a final silent **e** in many verbs. | make → making; write → writing | The silent **e** disappears before **-ing**. |
| Double the final consonant in the appropriate stressed, short-vowel pattern. | run → running; sit → sitting; begin → beginning | The final consonant is doubled to preserve the short vowel where the spelling pattern requires it. |
| Change final **-ie** to **-y**. | lie → lying; die → dying | These forms end in **-ying**, not *-ieing*. |
| Do not assume every final **e** disappears. | see → seeing; be → being | These are exceptions to the simplified silent-**e** rule. |

The doubling rule is not universal. For example, **open → opening**, not *openning*, because the word's stress and spelling pattern do not call for doubling. It is better to recognize familiar patterns than to mechanically double every consonant.

### 1.4 Use 1 — Actions happening at the moment of speaking

This is the most immediate use of the tense. The speaker describes something unfolding **right now**.

> **“Listen! Someone is knocking at the door.”**

The word **Listen!** directs attention to an action taking place at this moment. Similarly, **“Look! It is raining”** describes rain that the speaker can currently observe.

Time expressions such as **now, right now, at the moment,** and **currently** can make the meaning explicit. They are helpful but not mandatory: **“She's talking on the phone”** already suggests a present activity unless context says otherwise.

**Common mistake:** A learner might say *“Look! He plays football”* while pointing at somebody on the field. **“Look! He is playing football”** is more natural when describing the action currently unfolding. The simple present **“He plays football”** normally describes a habit, role, or general fact.

### 1.5 Use 2 — An activity continuing around the present

The action need not be happening at the precise moment of speech. It may be in progress throughout a **larger, unfinished period**.

> **“I'm reading *1984* at the moment.”**

The speaker might be talking to a friend instead of physically reading at that second. The sentence still works because the book is an ongoing reading project.

Other examples include:

- **“I'm learning AWS this week.”** The learning is taking place during the current period.
- **“Jimmy isn't performing well this semester.”** The speaker is describing a temporary pattern of performance during an unfinished semester.
- **“She's working on a new album.”** The work is an ongoing project, even if she is taking a break right now.

This is one of the most important points from Unit 1: **present continuous describes an action or situation in progress, not necessarily a nonstop action.**

### 1.6 Use 3 — Temporary situations

Present continuous often emphasizes that a situation is **temporary**, rather than the person's permanent or usual state.

Compare:

- **“He lives in Delhi.”** This sounds like his normal place of residence.
- **“He is living in Delhi for three months.”** This emphasizes a temporary arrangement.

Similarly, **“I'm working from home this week”** says that remote work is the current arrangement, without claiming that it is a permanent job condition.

Temporary periods are frequently introduced by **today, this week, this month, these days,** or **this year**. The situation may repeat throughout that period, but the speaker views it as a limited, developing, or current arrangement rather than an enduring habit.

**Common mistake:** Learners sometimes think that because a sentence contains **this year**, it must use a future tense. But **“Tod is playing for the football team this year”** can correctly describe his participation during the current season.

### 1.7 Use 4 — Situations that are changing or developing

The present continuous can describe a **process of change**.

- **“The weather is getting colder.”** The temperature is changing over time.
- **“Her English is improving.”** Her ability is developing.
- **“More people are using online learning platforms.”** The sentence presents a trend currently in progress.

Here, the point is not one isolated action. The speaker is describing a process whose state is evolving.

**Common mistake:** *“The weather gets colder”* is not inherently wrong, but it usually sounds like a general or repeated phenomenon. **“The weather is getting colder”** presents a change taking place during the current period.

### 1.8 Extension — Present continuous for definite future arrangements

> **Important distinction:** This usage arose from today's sentence discussion, but it belongs more fully to the book's later treatment of present tenses used for the future (commonly **Unit 19**). It is included here as a carefully labelled extension, not as though every detail of future tenses was formally studied in Unit 1.

The present continuous can describe a **future event that has already been arranged, scheduled, or planned with some degree of commitment**. The sentence uses a present grammatical form, but a future time expression or established conversation context indicates that the event will happen later.

> **“She is singing at the event tomorrow.”**

This does **not** mean she is singing right now. It indicates that tomorrow's performance is an existing arrangement. The same pattern appears in **“I'm meeting my professor on Monday”**, **“We're travelling home on Sunday”**, and **“They're playing a match next weekend.”**

The future interpretation is clearest when a phrase such as **tomorrow, next week, at six o'clock,** or **on Saturday** establishes the time. Without that context, **“She is singing at the event”** may be understood as describing an event happening now, although it can still refer to the future if both speakers already know which future event is being discussed.

#### Present continuous compared with *be going to*

Both forms can refer to the future, but they do not highlight exactly the same aspect of the speaker's intention.

- **“She is singing at the event tomorrow.”** This naturally presents the performance as an arrangement already in place.
- **“She is going to sing at the event tomorrow.”** This naturally presents the performance as an intention or plan; it does not by itself emphasize the practical arrangements as strongly.
- **“She sings at the event tomorrow.”** This simple-present form is less typical for a personal arrangement, but can occur when discussing an established programme or timetable.

These are **tendencies**, not absolute barriers: speakers often use *going to* even for fully arranged plans, and context usually matters more than a mechanical rule.

Consider also **“I'm going to work today.”** This sentence has two possible readings: it might mean **“I plan to do some work today”** (*going to* + the verb *work*), or it might mean **“I'm travelling to my workplace today”** (*going* as a movement verb, with *to work* indicating destination). If the intended meaning is simply that work is on your schedule, **“I'm working today”** is often clearer.

**Common mistake:** It is incorrect to assume that present continuous can *never* refer to the future because its name says *present*. It is equally misleading to assume that every present-continuous sentence is a future plan. **Context and time references determine the interpretation.**

### 1.9 Present continuous versus present simple: the basic contrast

For now, keep the contrast focused on meaning. The present simple is treated in its own later unit; the purpose here is to avoid confusing an ongoing activity with a usual fact.

| Present continuous | Present simple |
| --- | --- |
| **“I am studying English.”** I am engaged in this activity now or during this period. | **“I study English.”** Studying English is a habitual or general activity. |
| **“She is working from home this week.”** This describes a current temporary arrangement. | **“She works from home.”** This describes her ordinary arrangement. |
| **“The number of users is increasing.”** The number is changing now. | **“The number of users increases every December.”** This describes a repeated pattern. |

Do not select the tense simply because a sentence contains a particular time phrase. Ask whether the speaker means **an unfolding or temporary situation**, or **a usual, repeated, or general fact**.

Some common verbs describing states—such as **know, believe, own,** and **understand**—do not ordinarily use the continuous form in their usual stative meanings. Thus **“I understand the question”** is normally better than *“I am understanding the question.”* The detailed rules for state verbs can be studied with the unit that compares present continuous and present simple, rather than treating this brief caution as the full rule.

### 1.10 Corrections and observations from today's writing exercise

These examples are worth preserving because they distinguish **grammatical tense**, **natural word choice**, and **intended meaning**—three things that are sometimes confused.

**Example 1 — “I'm going to work today.”** The sentence is correct, but ambiguous between a planned activity and physical movement to a workplace. A clearer expression of an arranged workday is **“I'm working today.”** The original sentence is not grammatically wrong.

**Example 2 — “She is singing in the event.”** The present-continuous construction **is correct**. When referring to participation in a performance, **“at the event”** usually sounds more natural than **“in the event”**. To communicate the intended future arrangement unambiguously, write **“She is singing at the event tomorrow.”** This is a preposition and context refinement, not a tense correction.

**Example 3 — “Tod is playing in the football team this year.”** The tense is appropriate for his participation during the current season. **“In the team”** is natural in British English, while **“on the team”** is more common in American English. **“Playing for the football team”** also works well.

**Example 4 — “Jimmy is not performing well this semester.”** This is correct. **This semester** describes an unfinished period during which the situation is being observed.

**Example 5 — “Aren't u coming along with us?”** The tense and negative-question structure are correct. In standard written English, spell the pronoun **you**, rather than the informal texting abbreviation **u**. The polished sentence is **“Aren't you coming along with us?”**

### 1.11 A short checklist for checking your own sentences

When a sentence sounds uncertain, test its meaning and its form in this order.

1. **Identify the intended time and meaning.** Is the activity unfolding now, continuing during this period, changing over time, or arranged for the future?
2. **Choose the correct form of *be*.** Match *am*, *is*, or *are* to the subject.
3. **Form the main verb with *-ing*.** Check silent **e**, consonant doubling, and final **-ie** where relevant.
4. **Check word order.** Statements normally begin with the subject; questions ordinarily put the auxiliary before the subject.
5. **Check natural wording.** Correct grammar does not guarantee the most idiomatic preposition or phrase.
6. **Check whether the time is obvious.** Future-arrangement uses are often clearer with **tomorrow**, **next week**, or similar context.

The central principle is straightforward: the present continuous depicts an activity or situation as **in progress**, whether that progress is happening right now or across a temporary period. Its future-arrangement use extends that form to events that are already planned.

## Unit 1 — Mini practice with explanations

**Question 1.** Complete: “Listen! Someone _____ (knock) at the door.”

**Answer: “is knocking.”** The listener is being asked to notice an action happening right now. The subject **someone** is singular, so the auxiliary is **is**.

**Question 2.** Correct: “Why you are wearing a suit today?”

**Answer: “Why are you wearing a suit today?”** A direct question needs the auxiliary **are** before the subject **you**.

**Question 3.** Choose the intended meaning: “She is singing at the concert next Saturday.”

**Answer:** This can describe a future arrangement. **Next Saturday** supplies the future time, and the present continuous suggests the performance is scheduled or otherwise arranged.

**Question 4.** Correct: “Jimmy doesn't performing well this semester.”

**Answer: “Jimmy isn't performing well this semester.”** The negative present continuous uses **is not** (or **isn't**) before the **-ing** form; **doesn't** belongs to a different verb pattern.

**Question 5.** Explain the difference between “I'm reading a book” and “I read books.”

**Answer:** The first presents reading as an activity currently in progress or ongoing during this period. The second expresses a general habit or characteristic.

## 2. Present Simple (I do) — Unit 2

> **Studied on 10 October 2026:** Raymond Murphy, *English Grammar in Use*, Unit 2. The explanation and exercises were completed, the answers were checked, and five original sentences were reviewed. This section records the rules needed for the unit and the mistakes worth remembering.

### 2.1 Formal definition and central meaning

The **present simple tense**, also called the **simple present**, describes situations presented as **usual, repeated, generally true, or existing as a state**. Unlike the present continuous, it does not normally draw attention to an action unfolding at this exact moment.

For example, **“He plays football every weekend”** describes a repeated habit. It tells us what he normally does, not whether he is playing at the moment of speaking. **“Water boils at about 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure”** states a general fact under specified conditions. **“Mahesh seems friendly”** expresses a present impression or state, not a physical action being performed.

The defining idea is therefore **habitual or general validity**, not merely “something that happens in the present.” A sentence may describe a daily routine, a long-term preference, an occupation, a fact, or an opinion without implying that the activity is taking place right now.

### 2.2 Formal sentence structure

For most verbs, the present simple uses the **base form** with **I, you, we, and they**, but adds a **third-person singular ending** with **he, she, and it** or a singular noun.

| Subject | Affirmative example | Verb form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I / you / we / they | **They live** in Bengaluru. | Base form: **live** |
| He / she / it | **He lives** in Whitefield. | Third-person singular: **lives** |
| Singular noun | **Mahesh seems** friendly. | Third-person singular: **seems** |

**Affirmative pattern:** **Subject + base verb** (add **-s / -es**, or change **-y** to **-ies**, for third-person singular where appropriate) + remainder of sentence.

**Negative pattern:** **Subject + do/does + not + base verb** + remainder of sentence.

**Yes/no question pattern:** **Do/Does + subject + base verb + … ?**

These patterns apply to ordinary lexical verbs such as *live, play, like,* and *work*. The verb **be** has its own forms—**am, is, are**—and ordinarily does **not** use *do* or *does* to form its negatives and questions: **“She isn't ready”** and **“Is she ready?”**, not *“She doesn't be ready.”*

### 2.3 Third-person singular: why *he goes* but *they go*

In the affirmative present simple, a **third-person singular subject** changes the verb form. This is one of the most common sources of otherwise small grammatical errors.

- Most verbs add **-s**: *like → likes, live → lives, seem → seems*.
- Verbs ending in **-s, -sh, -ch, -x**, and some ending in **-o**, normally add **-es**: *watch → watches, wash → washes, fix → fixes, go → goes*.
- With a **consonant + y**, change **y** to **-ies**: *study → studies, try → tries*.
- With a **vowel + y**, simply add **-s**: *play → plays, enjoy → enjoys*.
- **Have** becomes **has**: **“She has a car.”**

Compare **“I go to college”** with **“He goes to college.”** The action is the same, but **he** requires the third-person form. Likewise, **“Mahesh seems to be a good guy”** is correct because *Mahesh* is one person, so **seems** takes **-s**.

A common mistake is to choose the verb ending based on the closest nearby noun instead of the actual subject. Identify **who or what the sentence is about** before deciding whether the verb needs a singular ending.

### 2.4 Negatives: *don't* and *doesn't*

Ordinary present-simple negatives use the helping verb **do** or **does**, followed by **not**. In everyday English these are usually contracted to **don't** and **doesn't**.

**I / you / we / they + don't + base verb.** For example, **“They don't work on Sundays.”**

**He / she / it + doesn't + base verb.** For example, **“He doesn't like Honda as much as he likes Mitsubishi.”**

Notice the contrast inside that last sentence: **“doesn't like”** in the negative clause, but **“he likes”** in the affirmative clause. After **doesn't**, the main verb returns to its **base form** because **does** already marks the third-person singular subject.

**Common mistake:** *“He doesn't likes Honda.”* The correct form is **“He doesn't like Honda.”** Never attach the third-person **-s** to the main verb after *doesn't*.

For the same reason, *“She don't like it”* is not standard formal English: **she** requires **doesn't** in an ordinary present-simple negative.

### 2.5 Questions: *do/does* and word order

To ask an ordinary **yes/no question** in the present simple, put **do** or **does** before the subject and use the **base form** of the main verb.

- **“Do you live in Whitefield?”**
- **“Does he live in Whitefield?”**
- **“Does she like this book?”**

The structure of your own sentence, **“Does he live in Whitefield?”**, is entirely correct. *Does* marks third-person singular; *live* therefore has no **-s**.

For a question beginning with *where, why, what, when,* or *how*, the question word normally comes first:

**Question word + do/does + subject + base verb + … ?**

Thus, **“Where does Mahesh live?”** and **“Why does she dislike the film?”** are correct. *“Where does Mahesh lives?”* is not, because it places the singular ending on the main verb even though *does* already carries it.

Short answers repeat the auxiliary rather than the complete action: **“Yes, he does,” “No, he doesn't,” “Yes, they do,”** or **“No, they don't.”**

**Useful exception:** When **who** or **what** is itself the subject of a question, ordinary present-simple questions generally do **not** add *do/does*: **“Who lives here?”** and **“What makes that sound?”** This is different from **“Who does he know?”**, where *who* is the object rather than the subject.

### 2.6 The uses you need to recognize

**Habits and repeated actions.** The present simple is the usual choice when describing a routine or something that happens regularly: **“He goes to church every Sunday”** and **“I practise English in the evening.”** The sentence does not assert that the activity is happening at this exact moment.

**Facts and generally true statements.** The tense also states information that holds in general: **“The Earth revolves around the Sun”** or **“A triangle has three sides.”** These are not habits; they are facts. The present simple is therefore broader than descriptions of daily routines.

**Opinions, preferences, emotions, and states.** Verbs such as **like, love, prefer, know, believe, understand,** and **seem** commonly express states rather than ongoing activities. **“She loves him,” “He prefers Mitsubishi,”** and **“Mahesh seems friendly”** naturally use the present simple. These verbs are not all forbidden in continuous forms in every possible meaning or context, but their ordinary *state* meanings generally favour the simple tense.

**Long-term circumstances.** **“She works at a bank”** or **“He lives in Bengaluru”** describes a situation presented as regular or relatively stable. If the speaker wants to emphasize that a situation is temporary, the present continuous may be more natural: **“She's working from home this week.”** This difference is about the speaker's meaning, not a strict limit on how long a situation can last.

### 2.7 Common time expressions and adverbs of frequency

Words such as **always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely,** and **never** help describe how frequently an action happens. They commonly appear **before the main verb** but **after forms of *be***.

Compare **“She often reads before bed”** with **“She is often tired after work.”** In the first sentence, *often* precedes the main verb *reads*; in the second, it follows *is*.

Expressions such as **every day, every weekend, on Mondays,** and **once a week** also naturally accompany habits. They are clues to meaning, not commands to use one tense regardless of context.

A particularly useful writing distinction is:

- **Every day** (two words) means **each day**: **“He goes to church every day.”**
- **Everyday** (one word) is an **adjective** meaning **ordinary or routine**: **“These are my everyday shoes.”**

### 2.8 Present simple versus present continuous: the essential difference

The two forms do not merely represent different degrees of grammatical formality. They often present the **same activity from different viewpoints**.

**“She works from home.”** This suggests a normal or established arrangement.

**“She is working from home this week.”** This highlights a temporary situation around the present.

**“He plays football.”** This states a habit, ability, or regular participation.

**“He is playing football now.”** This describes an activity currently underway.

You do not need to memorize hundreds of time expressions to choose correctly. Ask yourself whether you are describing **what normally happens or is generally true**, or **what is unfolding or temporarily happening around now**. That question usually identifies the appropriate tense.

### 2.9 Corrections and observations from today's five sentences

**1. “He goes to the church everyday.”**

Your tense is correct: **he goes** follows the third-person rule. A more natural version for regular religious attendance is **“He goes to church every day.”** Here, *go to church* commonly means *attend a religious service*. **“Go to the church”** is also grammatical, but it more readily suggests going to a particular church **building**. Separately, **every day** must be written as two words when it means *each day*.

**2. “She loves him to heaven and hell.”**

The present-simple form **loves** is correct because *love* expresses a state or feeling. The awkwardness is idiomatic, not a tense error. If the intended meaning is **strong affection**, **“She loves him to the moon and back”** sounds more natural. If you mean **steadfast loyalty through difficulties**, **“She loves him through thick and thin”** expresses that different idea. These two alternatives are not exact synonyms; choose according to the meaning you want.

**3. “Mahesh seems to be a good guy.”**

This sentence is correct. **Seems** expresses an impression; it does not normally describe an action actively being performed. The third-person singular ending **-s** is also correct with *Mahesh*.

**4. “He doesn't like Honda as much as he likes Mitsubishi.”**

This sentence is correct and a good illustration of two related rules: **doesn't + like** uses the base form after the auxiliary, while **he likes** uses the third-person singular ending in the affirmative clause. The comparison **as much as** is also natural.

**5. “Does he live in Whitefield?”**

This question is correct. **Does** appears before the subject, and **live** remains in its base form. *“Does he lives … ?”* would be incorrect.

### 2.10 The rule to carry forward

The most useful single rule from this unit is that **third-person singular marking appears once** in ordinary present-simple clauses: on the main verb in an affirmative sentence (**“She likes it”**), but on **does** in a negative or question (**“She doesn't like it”; “Does she like it?”**).

A final quick check before writing is to identify the **subject**, decide whether the statement describes a **habit/fact/state**, and then inspect the **verb form**. That method catches most of the errors addressed in Unit 2 without turning the tense into a long list of exceptions.
